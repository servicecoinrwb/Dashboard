Script · run top to bottom, every callbackThe 90-Second Callback

One continuous call, not a menu. Blue = say it. Red = the hook. If a call goes sideways and you only land two things, land the lock and the "deal?"

Open & claim0:00–0:20
Say it"Hey, this is Brian with Mechanical Temp — you just called about your AC. I can have a tech at your place between [time] and [time]. Want me to lock that in right now?"
Why: Owner calling back in 3 minutes with a real window. Quote a range anchored early — "9:30 to 11" beats "11." Lock first; equipment details come by text after.
Triage check0:20–0:35
Say it"Quick question — is the house getting dangerously hot? Anybody elderly, little kids, pets?"
Why: True emergency = highest-close, highest-ticket call of the day. Skip only when it's obviously not needed (commercial, or they already told you).
The lock — money down0:35–1:00
Say it"So the diagnostic visit is $89 — that's standard, and it comes straight off the repair. I just collect it at booking instead of at the door, because that's what locks your slot. Nobody bumps you. Texting you the link now."
Why: Same money, relocated — "standard" and "instead of at the door" make it timing, not an extra charge. Money down closes the auction: a customer with $89 committed doesn't take the 9:10 counter-offer. Whoever won't pay just told you they were always going to jump. Fallback if bookings stall: a $49 deposit — "deposit" sounds refundable, and sunk cost doesn't scale with dollars.
The guilt transfer1:00–1:20
The hook"One thing — I'm setting my whole morning around you now. If somebody else gets it fixed first, or you get it handled, no hard feelings at all — just shoot me a text so I don't drive out there for nothing. Deal?"
Why: Wait for them to actually say "deal" or "yeah." That word is the contract. Silent no-shows drop hard once they've promised a specific guy out loud.
The inoculation1:20–1:40
The hook"Heads up — you probably called a couple other places, so your phone's gonna ring for the next hour and some guy will say he can be there in 30 minutes. Most of those slip — that's just how they win the phone call. You're locked with me and I'll actually be there."
Why: You just predicted the exact counter-offer they're about to get. When it comes, it sounds like the sales tactic you called out — not a rescue. Evening version: "your phone might still ring tonight."
Evening variant (after 4p / weekends)Rate quote
Say it"I can have you looked at tonight — evenings run at our emergency rate. Or first thing tomorrow at the standard rate. Which works for you?"
Why: Let them pick the premium — it never feels like gouging when tomorrow-cheaper was on the table. Most no-cools in a hot house pick tonight. If the part's not on the truck: collect the diagnostic at the door, get the quote signed, part return is tomorrow's first slot — a pre-sold job, not a maybe.
Read the closeListen
"Sounds great" + silence = backup plan. Committed customers ask questions — "do you take card," "what's the fee," "should I do anything before you get here." Instant polite agreement with zero questions means they're back on Google the second you hang up. Push the payment link harder, or mark the slot soft.

Why it worksThe Two Bursts

Emergency demand isn't flat — it's two spikes: wake-up-hot (7–10a) and come-home-hot (4–7p), because discovery only happens when someone's home. Weekends, people are home all day — demand runs continuous while half the vans in Metro Detroit are parked.

So the schedule puts trucks where the calls are, the break where they aren't, emergency rates where competition is thin, and the ad budget on the same curve. Same total hours as the old 8–4 grind — placed where the money is.

Burst Dispatch™ · The MT MethodThe Schedule We Invented Over a Heat Wave

Every HVAC company in Metro Detroit runs the same schedule: two trucks, 8 to 4, Monday through Friday, weekends for emergencies you grumble about. We looked at when customers actually break down — and rebuilt the whole week around their clock instead of ours.

"Same hours as everyone else — just placed where the money is."

01 · The problemThe Rat Race Is a Timing Problem

An emergency no-cool customer calls four companies and hires whoever commits to the earliest credible arrival. That auction stays open until a truck is physically in the driveway. Call back in three minutes and quote 1pm? You lose to the guy quoting 10:30 — the fight is lost at scheduling, not at the phone.

And the calls don't trickle in. They come in two waves — wake-up-hot (7–10am) and come-home-hot (4–7pm) — because people only discover a dead AC when they're home. Weekends, they're home all day: demand runs continuous while half the market's vans sit parked.

02 · The methodFive Rules

1Complete & Restart. Mornings are pure emergency calls at regular rate — diagnose, fix, invoice, done by 1. Then a hard break. Then a fresh restart at 4 for the second wave. Same total hours as the 8–4 grind, placed where the calls are.

2The dead zone is a firewall. 1–4pm holds only droppable work — FM, parts, office. Other shops stagger techs and feed them overflow until the evening guy is cooked by 5. Our evening truck arrives at the second burst rested, with an empty board. That's the product.

3Premium hours are the best inventory, not the punishment. After 4pm and all weekend, competition thins out and rates go to emergency pricing — quoted straight, with "tomorrow at standard rate" always on the table so the customer picks the premium themselves.

4The ad budget rides the same curve. LSA spend throttled low on contested weekday mornings, cranked on underpriced weekends, paused the moment the board is full. Buy leads where you win them.

5Money down closes the auction. Diagnostic collected at booking, confirm texts before rolling, "text me if you get it handled — deal?" on every call. Ghosts and dry runs go to nearly zero, and a booked customer stops shopping.

03 · The moatWhy Nobody Copies This

It's not a secret — it's a trade the big shops structurally can't take. Their techs expect 8–4. Their dashboards punish empty morning slots as wasted utilization. Their culture treats on-call as the misery rotation you dump on the new guy. Changing any of that in a ten-truck shop is a mutiny; in a two-man shop where one man owns the P&L, it's a Tuesday decision.

"Everyone can see the weekend is underserved. Almost no one is organizationally shaped to serve it."

04 · For the techWhat's In It for the Man in the Truck

Weekdays barely change: 8–4, clean morning service calls, FM in the afternoon. The upgrade is the weekend: every weekend ticket bills at emergency rates, and the tech working it earns a cut of every ticket he writes — money that only exists because we're open when nobody else is. A good hot Saturday should beat two regular weekdays, and when you're off, you're actually off: the other truck has it, no phone.

05 · The proofRun It Like a Trade

The Simulator tab runs both schedules against identical customers, 12 simulated months at a time — the burst model has never lost a configuration, sandbagged or not. But a backtest is a hypothesis, not a fill. So the rollout is an experiment: playbook on every call, weekend LSA on, one log line per lead, two weeks. Then the log recalibrates the model with real numbers and the schedule earns its next decision.
